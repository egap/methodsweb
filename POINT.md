# The point of the EGAP methods site

The methods site of Evidence in Governance and Politics (EGAP),
methods.egap.org, hosts short guides explaining methods of impact evaluation.
Most of the guides are about randomized experiments. Each guide is about one
topic, such as statistical power or pre-analysis plans, and is organized as
the ten things a reader most needs to know about it. All 33 guides are in
English. Eleven of them are also in Spanish and French.

The readers are people who need to use or judge a method soon and who are
not methods specialists: academic researchers and students designing a
study, monitoring and evaluation staff at partner organizations, and
policymakers who read evaluations. Because policymakers and partner staff
read the guides too, a guide has to be readable by someone without training
in mathematical statistics. After reading a guide, a reader should be able
to say what the method is for, when it applies, and what can go wrong with
it. A reader who wants to try the method should be able to run the guide's R
code on their own computer after installing packages from CRAN alone. The
source of every guide, R code included, is public on GitHub
(egap/methodsweb). So a reader can also check how a result was computed and
can propose a correction through an issue or a pull request.

EGAP wants to improve the rigor of impact evaluations and to make existing
methods accessible to people outside academic statistics. It also wants the
people who read evaluations to be able to tell a strong design from a weak
one.

The guides are not a course or a textbook. For that purpose EGAP published *The
Theory and Practice of Field Experiments*, a book built from the materials of
its Learning Days workshops. The site's Teaching page links to the book.
Because a guide covers one topic in ten points, it leaves out much of what a
specialist would say about that topic. Authors should recommend a method in a
guide only as broadly as the evidence for that method supports.
